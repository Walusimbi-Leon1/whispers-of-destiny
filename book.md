### A Novel

**By Walusimbi Leon (SGSS)**

> *"The heart has its reasons, which reason knows nothing of."*
> — Blaise Pascal

## Prologue — The Photograph

The photograph was never meant to be found.

It sat in a dusty cardboard box beneath David Ochieng's bed, buried under old notebooks and forgotten receipts, wrapped in a faded kikoi cloth that smelled of dried lake salt and years gone by. David had packed it a dozen times, moving from one rented room to another across Nairobi, always telling himself he would throw it away. But he never did.

On this particular evening, three months before his life would change forever, he found himself kneeling on the concrete floor of his apartment in Kitisuru, the box open before him, the kikoi unwrapped in his hands.

The photograph showed a woman with a wide, easy smile and eyes that held the warmth of an African sunset. She stood on the shores of Lake Turkana, the jade-green water stretching behind her like a promise, her arm raised in greeting or farewell — David could never decide which. He had taken this photograph on the second anniversary of their meeting, and it was the last good picture he ever took of her.

Her name was Amara.

David had first met Amara at a gallery opening in Nairobi, a showcase of local photographers where he had been invited to display some of his work. She had come with a friend, had spent a long time studying his photographs, and then had introduced herself with a directness that had surprised him. "You see things," she had said. "Not just landscapes. You see the stories in them."

They had fallen in love quickly, the way young people do when they don't yet understand the weight of their choices. She had moved into his apartment within three months, and for a year, they had been happy — genuinely happy. He had photographed her in every light, every landscape, every mood. She had been his muse, his partner, his confidante.

But the cracks had appeared slowly, like hairline fractures in a foundation. She had wanted a ring. He had wanted more time. She had wanted a house. He had wanted to travel. She had wanted children. He had wanted — what had he wanted? He had wanted to keep things exactly as they were, suspended in the amber of their young love, unchanged and unchanging.

When she had finally left, she had not screamed or cried. She had packed her bags methodically, had called her sister to pick her up, and had looked at him with something like pity. "You love your camera more than you love me," she had said, and it was the truest thing she had ever spoken.

David traced the outline of her face with his thumb, the paper soft and worn from years of this same ritual. He remembered the sound of her laughter, the way it carried across the savannah like a bird taking flight. He remembered the way she looked at him when she told him she was leaving, her eyes dry and determined, his own vision blurring with tears he refused to shed.

"You love your camera more than you love me," she had said, and the accusation had cut deeper because there was truth in it.

Amara had wanted a life of certainty — a house in the suburbs, children who would grow up speaking proper English, weekends spent at the club in Nairobi. She had wanted a man who came home at six, not one who disappeared into the bush for weeks, chasing the perfect shot of a lion at dawn or a migration crossing that might never come. She had wanted David to be someone else, and when he couldn't become that person, she had found someone who could.

David put the photograph back in the box and closed the lid.

Outside his window, the sun was setting over the Ngong Hills, painting the sky in shades of orange and purple that would have been beautiful if he had thought to capture them. But some beauty, he had learned, was not meant to be held. Some beauty was meant to pass through you like wind, leaving nothing but memory in its wake.

He had not loved anyone since Amara. He had not allowed himself to.

Three thousand miles away, in a small flat in South London, Sarah Nakato was staring at her own reflection in a hotel bathroom mirror, wondering if she was about to make the biggest mistake of her life.

The mistake was not the trip to Nairobi. The trip was work, a project she had been preparing for months, a museum commission that would be the crowning achievement of her career if she could pull it off. The mistake was not the engagement ring on her finger, a flawless diamond that had cost Robert a small fortune and that she wore like a promise she was no longer sure she wanted to keep.

The mistake was that she was not sure she loved Robert anymore.

Or rather, she was not sure she had ever loved him the way a woman should love the man she was going to marry.

Robert was kind. Robert was successful. Robert was everything a Ugandan mother could want for her daughter — a banker with a corner office, a man who remembered anniversaries and sent flowers on Valentine's Day. He was safe and stable and predictable, and for three years, Sarah had told herself that this was enough. That love was a decision, not a feeling. That the grand passion she read about in novels was a fantasy that sensible women outgrew.

But tonight, on the eve of her flight to Nairobi, she lay awake in the darkness of her flat, staring at the ceiling, and felt something she had not felt in years.

The feeling was possibility. The feeling was the terrifying, exhilarating certainty that somewhere out there, her real life was waiting to begin.

She did not know that she would meet David Ochieng on a plane the next morning — a man who carried a camera bag and the scent of sandalwood, whose eyes were the color of honey and whose voice would unlock something in her she had kept buried for years. She did not know that the photograph under his bed was about to find its match in her heart. She did not know that destiny, that old and patient weaver, was about to pull the threads of their separate lives together in a pattern neither of them could have foreseen.

She only knew that when she closed her eyes, she saw not Robert's face, but a landscape she had never visited — golden plains stretching to an endless horizon, acacia trees silhouetted against a crimson sky, and the whisper of a wind that carried the scent of rain and adventure and everything she had been too afraid to want.

## Chapter 1 — The Meeting

The Kenya Airways flight from Heathrow to Nairobi was half empty, which was unusual for a Tuesday in June. Sarah found herself with an entire row to herself, three seats across which she could stretch out and pretend to sleep, though sleep had never come easily to her on airplanes. There was something about being suspended above the earth at thirty-five thousand feet that made her want to stay awake, to be present for the miracle of flight, to watch the darkness and the stars and the tiny lights of distant cities.

She had dressed comfortably for the journey — loose-fitting linen trousers, a soft cotton sweater, flat shoes that slipped easily on and off for security. Her hair was pulled back in a simple bun, and she wore no makeup except a touch of lip balm. She looked, she thought, exactly like what she was: a woman traveling alone, focused on the work ahead, uninterested in performing for anyone.

The man who sat down beside her just before the doors closed was carrying a camera bag that looked heavy enough to contain the entire Serengeti.

He was tall, with broad shoulders that seemed to fill the space around him, and skin the color of rich earth after rain. His face was angular, almost severe in repose, with high cheekbones and a jaw that spoke of stubbornness. But his eyes — she noticed his eyes immediately — were the warmest shade of brown she had ever seen, the color of honey caught in sunlight, and they held a gentleness that seemed at odds with the rest of him.

"Sorry," he said, maneuvering the camera bag into the overhead compartment. "I should have checked this. I always say I'll check it, and then I never do."

"It's fine," Sarah said, pulling her feet back to give him room. "I've had my bag up there for the last ten minutes. I'm in no hurry."

He settled into the seat beside her, and she caught the scent of him — sandalwood and something earthier, like wood smoke and open air. He smelled like the places she had only ever seen in photographs.

"David," he said, extending his hand.

"Sarah." She took his hand, and the feel of his palm against hers was warm and dry and sent a small, unexpected current through her arm.

"First time in Nairobi?" he asked.

"Is it that obvious?"

"Only because you said 'Nairobi' the way people say 'adventure.' Nairobians say it like they're coming home." He smiled, and the smile transformed his face, softening the angular lines and revealing a boyishness that seemed incongruous for a man his size.

"That's very perceptive," Sarah said. "Are you a psychologist as well as a photographer?"

"Just a photographer. But I've spent a lot of time watching people. You learn to read them."

"Read me, then."

He tilted his head, studying her with an intensity that should have been uncomfortable but wasn't. "You're a professional — something creative, I think. An architect, maybe. You've been working too hard, and you're running from something. Or to something. I can't tell which yet."

Sarah felt the breath catch in her throat. "That's — that's remarkably accurate."

"Lucky guess." He shrugged, but his eyes were still watching her, and she had the unsettling feeling that he could see through her carefully constructed composure to the uncertainty underneath.

"I am an architect," she said. "I'm heading to Nairobi for a project — a museum commission."

"A museum. That sounds important."

"It is. The Kazuri Museum of East African Culture. The firm I work for won the bid last year, and I've been leading the design team. This is the site visit — final approvals, meeting with the local contractors, making sure everything is on track."

"Kazuri," David repeated. "That means 'small and beautiful' in Swahili. It's a fitting name."

"You speak Swahili?"

"Some. I'm Luo, but I grew up in Nairobi. You pick up a bit of everything. What about you? You're Ugandan, aren't you?"

Surprise flickered across her face. "How did you know?"

"Your accent, mostly. But also —" He paused, searching for the words. "There's something about the way Ugandans carry themselves. A certain warmth. Kenyans are more formal, more reserved. Ugandans greet you like they've known you all your life."

"I haven't been back in six years," Sarah said, and she heard the wistfulness in her own voice. "My family is still there — my mother and father, my younger brother. I visit when I can, but it's never enough."

"Where in Uganda?"

"Kampala. Well, I grew up there. My parents are from the village — Jinja, near the source of the Nile — but they moved to the city before I was born."

"The source of the Nile," David said, and his voice carried a note of reverence. "I've never been, but I've always wanted to see it. There's something sacred about the idea of a river beginning. A starting point for everything that follows."

Sarah nodded slowly. "That's beautiful. And true. Every time I visit, I feel — connected. To something larger than myself."

The flight attendants began the safety demonstration, and they fell silent, watching the practiced choreography of pointing hands and inflating life vests. But the silence between them was comfortable, natural, as if they had known each other for years rather than minutes.

When the plane began its taxi toward the runway, David turned to her again.

"Do you believe in fate?" he asked.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I've never really thought about it. Do you?"

"Every day." He looked out the window at the gray English sky, and his reflection in the glass was thoughtful. "I spend my life waiting for moments — the right light, the right angle, the right second when everything comes together. A lion yawning at sunset, a cheetah stretching after a kill, a child laughing in a village market. Those moments don't happen by accident. They happen because the universe conspires to create them."

"And you think the same is true of — of meetings? Of people?"

"I think everything is connected," he said. "I think we're all part of a story that's being written, and we don't get to see the full picture. We only get to trust that the thread we're following leads somewhere meaningful."

The plane accelerated, pressing them back into their seats, and the roar of the engines filled the cabin. Through the window, the runway markings blurred and disappeared as the ground fell away beneath them.

Sarah looked at David's profile, silhouetted against the brightening sky, and felt something shift inside her — a door opening, a curtain parting, the first whisper of a story she had not known she was waiting to hear.

## Chapter 2 — Strangers in Transit

The flight was nine hours, but it felt like minutes.

They talked through the meal service, through the in-flight movie neither of them watched, through the long darkness over the Mediterranean and the Sahara. They talked about their work — David's photography, her architecture — and discovered a shared philosophy about both. They believed that true craftsmanship was invisible, that the best work made people feel something before they understood why. They believed that beauty was not decoration but necessity, that well-designed spaces and well-captured moments could change the way people saw the world.

"You should see the Mara," David said, leaning toward her across the middle seat that separated them. "The Maasai Mara in October, when the wildebeest migration is at its peak. The herds stretch from horizon to horizon, millions of animals moving as one organism. The dust rises like smoke from a massive fire. The ground trembles beneath their hooves. It's the closest thing to witnessing creation that I've ever experienced."

"With the right light," Sarah said, echoing a phrase he had used earlier.

"Yes." He smiled. "Always with the right light."

"And do you always find it? The right light?"

"Not always. Sometimes I wait for days, for weeks. Sometimes I miss the shot entirely and have to come back the following year." He paused, considering. "But the waiting is part of it. The anticipation. The knowing that the moment will come, and when it does, you have to be ready."

"Is that a philosophy of photography or of life?"

"Both," he said. "Photography taught me everything I know about life. Patience. Attention. The courage to wait for what matters instead of settling for what's easy."

Sarah thought about Robert, about the engagement ring on her finger, about the future she had planned with the methodical precision of a building design. She thought about the way she had chosen certainty over passion, security over risk, and wondered if she had been settling for what was easy.

"What about you?" David asked. "What did architecture teach you?"

"To see the bones of things," she said slowly. "When I look at a building, I don't see the facade — I see the structure underneath, the frame that holds everything up. I see the decisions the architect made, the compromises, the places where beauty was sacrificed for function and function was sacrificed for beauty. I see the story of how the building came to be."

"And people? Do you see the structure underneath when you look at people?"

Sarah considered the question. It was the kind of question a therapist might ask, or a poet. It was not the kind of question she was used to answering.

"I used to think I did," she said. "I used to think I was a good judge of character. But lately —" She stopped, uncertain how much to reveal to a stranger on a plane.

"Lately?"

"I'm not sure I know myself as well as I thought I did. And if I don't know myself, how can I possibly know anyone else?"

The lights in the cabin dimmed, signaling the overnight hours. Around them, other passengers were settling into sleep, pulling blankets over their shoulders and adjusting neck pillows. The soft hum of the engines filled the space between words.

"You don't have to talk about it," David said gently. "I'm not trying to pry."

"You're not prying. I just — I'm engaged." She held up her left hand, and the diamond caught the dim cabin light, scattering sparks across the ceiling. "I'm supposed to be getting married in December."

"Supposed to be?"

"We haven't set a date, exactly. We've been engaged for almost a year. Robert — my fiancé — wants a big wedding, the kind with three hundred guests and a sit-down dinner and a band that plays until morning. I want —" She stopped again, unsure what she wanted.

"What do you want?" David asked.

It was the simplest question in the world, and Sarah had no answer.

"I want to feel something," she said finally, and the admission felt like a confession. "I want to wake up in the morning and feel like my life is my own. I want to look at the person beside me and know, without a doubt, that I chose him — not because he was safe, not because he was what my parents wanted, but because I couldn't imagine my life without him. I want to feel the way you describe the Mara. Like I'm witnessing creation."

David was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was soft, almost tender.

"I felt that way once," he said. "Her name was Amara. She was a teacher at a school in Nairobi — she taught English to children who had never spoken a word of it. She had this laugh that made everyone around her smile, even when they didn't know what was funny. And she looked at me like I was the most important person in the world."

"What happened?"

"She left. She said I loved my work more than I loved her, and she was right. I did. I didn't know how to be present, how to choose her over the next photograph, the next assignment, the next adventure. I was so afraid of being trapped that I couldn't see I was trapping myself in loneliness."

"Are you still in love with her?"

David shook his head slowly. "I don't think so. I think I'm in love with the memory of who I was when I was with her. The person I might have become if I had made different choices. But that person doesn't exist anymore. He's as much a photograph as the ones in my portfolio."

They sat in silence for a while, the weight of their shared confessions settling around them like a blanket. Outside the window, the sky was beginning to lighten, the first pale glow of dawn spreading across the horizon.

"We should probably try to sleep," Sarah said, though she felt no desire to close her eyes.

"We should," David agreed, but he made no move to adjust his seat or reach for his blanket.

Instead, they sat side by side in the growing light, watching the African continent rise to meet them through the window — first the vast, rust-colored expanse of the Sahara, then the green patchwork of the Sahel, then the broken landscape of the Rift Valley, etched with ancient volcanoes and shimmering lakes.

"This is my favorite part of the journey," David said. "The approach. When you can see the land taking shape below you, and you know that in an hour, you'll be standing on it, feeling the sun on your skin and the red dust on your shoes."

"It's beautiful," Sarah breathed.

"It's home," he said. "Well, one of them. I don't really have a home anymore. I travel so much that I've learned to make home wherever I am. A hotel room in Mombasa, a tent in the Serengeti, a borrowed flat in Cape Town. Home is a feeling, not a place."

"That sounds lonely."

"Sometimes. But it also sounds free." He turned to look at her, his honey-brown eyes meeting hers. "Freedom has a cost, Sarah. Every choice we make closes off other possibilities. The question isn't whether we'll lose something — we always will. The question is whether what we gain is worth what we give up."

The captain's voice crackled over the intercom, announcing their descent into Nairobi. The seatbelt sign illuminated with a soft chime.

Sarah fastened her seatbelt and watched the city emerge below — the jumble of corrugated roofs and gleaming high-rises, the green expanse of the Karura Forest, the long ribbon of the Nairobi-Mombasa highway stretching toward an endless horizon.

She had arrived in Nairobi a different person than the one who had left London. She just didn't know it yet.

## Chapter 3 — Streets of Nairobi

Jomo Kenyatta International Airport was a study in controlled chaos. The arrivals hall teemed with travelers and greeters, taxi drivers holding handwritten signs, families reuniting with embraces and exclamations of joy. The air was thick with the scent of diesel fumes, roasting coffee, and the particular mustiness of an African airport that had seen generations of arrivals and departures.

David walked beside Sarah through the crowd, his camera bag slung over one shoulder, his other hand holding his passport. He moved with the ease of someone who had navigated this particular chaos a thousand times, weaving through the press of bodies without slowing, without apologizing, without seeming to notice the noise and the heat and the press of humanity.

"Where are you staying?" he asked, raising his voice to be heard above the din.

"The Serena," Sarah said. "It's where the firm always puts us up."

"Good choice. It's close to the city center, and the gardens are beautiful." He paused at the exit, where the afternoon sunlight blazed through the glass doors. "Do you have a car waiting?"

"The hotel sent a driver. He should be outside."

They emerged into the brightness of a Nairobi afternoon, and Sarah felt the heat wash over her like a wave — not the damp, clinging heat of London in summer, but something drier, more alive, carrying the scent of earth and dust and blooming jasmine.

She stopped on the pavement, closing her eyes for a moment, letting the sun warm her face.

"I'd forgotten," she said. "The way the sun feels here. It's different. It's — intentional."

"That's exactly the word." David stood beside her, not looking at her but at the sky, at the distant outline of the Ngong Hills, at the hawks circling lazily overhead. "The sun in Africa doesn't just warm you. It fills you. It's like being touched by something ancient and sacred."

A driver in a crisp white uniform approached, holding a sign that read "Sarah Nakato — Serena Hotel."

"That's me," Sarah said, and she was surprised to feel a pang of disappointment. Their journey was over. She would get into the car, he would find his own way home, and the connection they had forged in the pressurized cabin of an airplane would dissolve into the ordinary business of their separate lives.

"It was nice to meet you, Sarah." David extended his hand, and she took it again, feeling that same current of warmth she had felt at the beginning of the flight.

"You too, David. Thank you for —" She gestured vaguely, unable to find the right words. "For the conversation. For the company."

"The door is open." He smiled. "If you ever want to see Nairobi through someone else's eyes, you know where to find me."

He released her hand and turned away, disappearing into the crowd with the ease of a man who belonged to this city, this continent, this light.

Sarah watched him go, then followed the driver to the waiting car.

The drive to the Serena Hotel took twenty minutes through the streets of Nairobi, and Sarah spent the entire journey with her face pressed to the window, drinking in the city like a woman dying of thirst.

The driver, a quiet man named Joseph, navigated the traffic with the practiced ease of someone who had spent decades on these roads. He pointed out landmarks as they passed — the Kenyatta International Conference Centre, a towering cylindrical building that dominated the skyline; the Parliament buildings, where a guard in a red coat stood at attention; the University of Nairobi, its campus sprawling across several blocks of the city center.

"That is where I studied," Joseph said, gesturing toward the university. "Economics. But driving pays better."

They crossed a roundabout where a massive statue of Jomo Kenyatta, the nation's founding president, stood with his hand raised in greeting or blessing. The roundabout was ringed with jacaranda trees whose fallen purple blossoms carpeted the road like a royal welcome.

"They plant jacarandas all over the city," Sarah observed, remembering a piece of trivia from her research. "Is it true they were introduced by the British?"

"Yes, madam. They are not native to Kenya. But they have made themselves at home, as so many things do here." Joseph smiled in the rearview mirror. "Like you, perhaps."

Sarah found herself smiling back. "Perhaps."

Nairobi was not what she had expected. She had anticipated something like Kampala — chaotic, vibrant, overwhelming. But Nairobi had a different energy. It was busier, more organized, more international. The billboards advertised international brands alongside local ones. The cars on the road were a mix of sleek Japanese sedans and battered matatus painted with slogans and portraits of politicians. The people on the sidewalks walked with purpose, their phones pressed to their ears, their briefcases swinging at their sides.

And yet, for all its modernity, Nairobi was unmistakably African. The jacaranda trees lining the streets were in bloom, their purple flowers carpeting the pavement like fallen confetti. The air carried the mingled scents of charcoal grills, ripe mangoes, and the red dust that seemed to coat everything. And everywhere, everywhere, there was the sky — huge and blue and impossibly close, as if the heavens had descended to touch the earth.

The Serena Hotel was an oasis of calm in the heart of the city. The lobby was cool and dark, furnished with traditional African art and comfortable sofas arranged around a central fireplace. The staff greeted her by name, handed her a cold towel and a glass of tamarind juice, and escorted her to a room overlooking the hotel's famous gardens.

Sarah closed the door behind her and leaned against it, exhaling a breath she felt she had been holding since Heathrow.

The room was beautiful — spacious and airy, with a four-poster bed draped in mosquito netting, a writing desk by the window, and a balcony that looked out over a lush tropical garden where birds called to each other in the soft afternoon light.

She walked to the balcony and stepped outside, resting her hands on the railing.

Doubt crept in as it always did in quiet moments. She had left London at a pivotal point — the museum design was at a stage where decisions needed her approval, Robert had wanted her to postpone the trip so they could finalize wedding venues, and her mother had called three times in the last week to ask if everything was "settled."

Everything was not settled. Sarah had a feeling that nothing would ever be settled again.

She pulled out her phone and saw that she had missed a call from Robert. She listened to his voicemail — his voice was warm, familiar, and spoke of dinner plans and weekend arrangements and the humdrum rhythms of a life she was beginning to realize she did not want.

She texted him: *Arrived safely. Hotel is beautiful. Call you tomorrow. Love you.*

The last two words felt hollow, like a promise she could no longer keep.

She put down the phone and looked out at the garden, at the birds swooping between the trees, at the security guard walking his slow perimeter, at the Kenyan flag fluttering on its pole. And she thought of David, of his honey-brown eyes and his patient voice, of the way he had said, "Do you believe in fate?" as if it were the most natural question in the world.

*I'm starting to,* she thought. *I'm starting to.*

## Chapter 4 — The Project

The Kazuri Museum of East African Culture was not yet a building. It was a vision, a set of blueprints, a construction site on a plot of land just outside Nairobi, in a neighborhood called Karen, named for Karen Blixen, the Danish author of *Out of Africa*.

Sarah woke early on her first morning in Nairobi, the jet lag settling into her bones but the excitement of the project overriding her fatigue. She dressed in a sharp white blouse and tailored navy trousers, pulled her hair into a professional chignon, and applied the careful makeup she wore when she needed to project confidence she didn't always feel.

The project manager, a Kenyan woman named Grace Wanjiku, met her in the hotel lobby. Grace was in her forties, with a firm handshake and a no-nonsense demeanor that Sarah immediately respected. She drove a sturdy Land Cruiser that had seen better days and handled the chaotic Nairobi traffic with the practiced ease of a veteran.

"The site is coming along well," Grace said as they navigated a roundabout where three matatus were engaged in a honking standoff. "The foundation is laid, the steel structure is up. The local craftsmen have been doing excellent work on the stonework — they're using a local granite that has this beautiful warm color when the afternoon sun hits it."

"I've seen the samples," Sarah said. "The photographs don't do it justice."

Grace smiled. "Photographs never do. You have to see things with your own eyes. Touch them. Feel them. That's what this museum is about — giving people a chance to experience East African culture the way it deserves to be experienced."

The site was impressive. The steel skeleton rose from the red earth like a promise, already suggesting the graceful curves and open spaces that Sarah had designed. The location was stunning — set on a gentle rise with views of the Ngong Hills in one direction and the expanse of the Rift Valley in the other. Acacia trees dotted the landscape, their flat-topped silhouettes providing shade for the workers who were busy with various stages of construction.

Sarah walked the site for hours, inspecting the foundation, testing the steel welds, talking to the foreman about the drainage system and the electrical routing. She climbed scaffolding to check the alignment of the main hall, her boots leaving prints in the red dust that coated everything.

She met the stoneworkers, a team of four men from a village in western Kenya, who were carving the local granite into the decorative panels that would line the entrance hall. The head stoneworker, an elderly man named Mzee Jomo, showed her the patterns they were chiseling — traditional motifs inspired by the art of the Kikuyu, the Luo, the Maasai, and the Swahili people.

"Each pattern tells a story," Mzee Jomo explained, his weathered hands tracing the grooves he had carved into the stone. "This one is the story of creation, how the first man and woman came down from the mountain. This one is the story of the harvest, the rain, the seasons. This one —" He paused, a smile crinkling the corners of his eyes. "This one is the story of two lovers who found each other against all odds."

Sarah stared at the pattern he indicated — two intertwined spirals that rose toward each other and merged at the top. "It's beautiful," she said. "How long did it take to carve?"

"Three weeks. But I have been carving this story my whole life. Every piece I make is the same story, told differently."

She moved on to meet the structural engineers, a young Ghanaian-Kenyan woman named Akinyi who had trained in Johannesburg and returned to Nairobi to work on projects that celebrated African design. They spent an hour reviewing the load calculations for the roof, the tensile strength of the steel beams, the integration of the solar panels that would power the museum's lighting and climate control.

"The roof is the hardest part," Akinyi said, spreading the blueprints across a makeshift table. "You've designed these sweeping curves that are beautiful but challenging to execute. We've had to custom-fabricate several of the support beams."

"Can we meet the deadline?"

Akinyi grinned. "We're African. We do our best work under pressure."

Sarah climbed scaffolding to check the alignment of the main hall, her boots leaving prints in the red dust that coated everything. From the top of the structure, she could see the whole site spread out below — the workers moving between tasks, the trucks carrying stone and steel, the gardens that were being planted around the perimeter. In the distance, the Ngong Hills rose against the sky, their slopes green from recent rains.

This place was going to be more than a museum. It was going to be a testament to the beauty and resilience of East African culture. And she was part of creating it.

"This is going to be magnificent," she said to Grace as they stood in what would become the main gallery. The space was vast and open, with a high ceiling and floor-to-ceiling windows that would eventually offer panoramic views of the surrounding landscape.

"It will be," Grace agreed. "But we have challenges. The rains are coming earlier than expected, and we need to get the roof on before they arrive. The local stone supplier has been having transportation issues. And I'm not sure the budget we allocated for the interactive exhibits is going to be enough."

They spent the afternoon in a meeting room at the site office, reviewing budgets and timelines and supply chains. Sarah made notes in her leather-bound journal — the same kind of detailed, methodical notes she had been making since architecture school.

But even as she focused on the practical details, part of her mind was elsewhere. She kept glancing out the window at the Ngong Hills, their soft green slopes rising against the impossibly blue sky. She kept thinking about what David had said about the Mara, about the wildebeest migration, about the dust rising like smoke from a massive fire.

She kept thinking about his eyes.

"Sarah?" Grace was looking at her expectantly.

"I'm sorry, I was —" She shook her head. "The jet lag. Could you repeat that?"

"I said we should have dinner tonight. There's a place I know near the city center — they do the best nyama choma in Nairobi. My treat."

"That sounds wonderful," Sarah said. "Thank you."

That evening, Sarah found herself at a bustling restaurant with Grace, eating grilled meat with her hands, washing it down with cold Tusker beer, and laughing at stories Grace told about her three children and her husband who was a safari guide and was gone more often than he was home.

The walls were canvas, and the floor was packed earth, and she could hear the sounds of the bush all around her — birds calling, insects chirping, the distant lowing of wildebeests. She had never felt so exposed, so vulnerable, so alive.

In the afternoon, David took her on a game drive. They followed dirt tracks through the long grass, past herds of zebras and topis and Grant's gazelles. They found a pride of lions resting in the shade of a large rock outcropping, the cubs tumbling over each other while the adults dozed.

Sarah watched through binoculars, her breath held, afraid that even the sound of her breathing would disturb the scene.

"They look so peaceful," she whispered.

"They are. This is their home. They have nothing to prove, nothing to fear, nothing to rush toward or away from. They simply exist, in perfect harmony with their world."

"It must be nice," Sarah said. "To exist like that. Without second-guessing, without wondering if you're making the right choices."

"It's a lesson the Mara teaches." David turned to look at her, his eyes steady. "The animals don't question their instincts. They don't worry about the future or regret the past. They trust the moment. They trust themselves."

"And what about humans? Can we learn to do that?"

"Some of us can." He paused. "Most of us don't know how to try."

As the sun began to set, David led her to a kopje — a rocky outcropping that rose above the plains. They climbed to the top and sat together, watching the sky transform.

The sun descended in a blaze of color — gold and orange and crimson, fading into purple and indigo as the first stars appeared. The sky seemed larger here, the heavens closer, as if the Mara had its own atmosphere, its own relationship with the cosmos.

"Do you believe that some moments are meant to happen?" Sarah asked softly.

"Yes," David said. "I do."

"And this —" She gestured at the landscape, the sky, the two of them sitting together on the rock. "This moment. Do you believe it was meant to happen?"

He turned to look at her, and his eyes reflected the last light of the sunset.

"Yes, Sarah. I believe that with all my heart."

He leaned toward her, and she felt herself leaning toward him, a magnetic pull she had no desire to resist.

Their lips met.

The kiss was soft, tentative, a question more than an answer. His hand found hers, and she felt the warmth of his palm, the steadiness of his presence. She closed her eyes and let herself be present in the moment, trusting it, the way the animals trusted.

When they pulled apart, the first stars were fully visible above them.

"I'm engaged," she whispered.

"I know."

"Nothing about this is simple."

"I know."

"And I don't know what I'm doing."

David smiled, his thumb tracing a gentle line across her knuckles.

"Neither do I. But I know that I don't want to pretend this isn't happening. I know that I want to be with you, for as long as you'll let me. And I know that whatever happens — when you go back to London, when you make your choice — I will be grateful for this. For every moment of it."

She leaned against his shoulder, and they sat together under the great African sky, two people suspended in the brief, beautiful space between the choices that would define their lives.

## Chapter 12 — Under African Skies

They spent three days in the Mara, and every hour was a gift.

The first morning, David woke her before dawn, a steaming cup of tea in his hand. The canvas of the tent glowed faintly with the approaching light. "The light is perfect," he said. "Get dressed. I want to show you something."

Sarah pulled on her clothes in the half-darkness, her fingers clumsy with the zippers and buttons. The air was cold against her skin, carrying the smell of dew and earth and wood smoke from a fire that Lekuta had built somewhere nearby. She emerged from the tent to find David waiting by the Land Rover, two steaming cups of tea in his hands, the eastern sky already paling above the treeline.

"I've been waiting for the right conditions for weeks," he said as they drove into the darkness. "Clear sky, no wind, the river at its lowest. Everything has to align."

They drove through the cool darkness, the headlights cutting a narrow path through the grass. Lekuta was with them, his eyes scanning the darkness with the practiced vigilance of a man who had spent his life in this landscape.

They stopped at a bend in the Mara River, where the banks were lined with ancient fig trees. The sky was beginning to lighten, the stars fading one by one as the sun approached.

"Watch the river," David said.

They waited in silence, the only sounds the gurgle of the water and the first calls of awakening birds. And then, slowly, the sun crested the horizon, and the world transformed.

The light hit the water, turning it to liquid gold. A hippo surfaced, its massive head breaking the surface with a snort that echoed across the river. A crocodile slid from the bank into the water, its movement so smooth it was almost invisible. And then, in a moment that Sarah would carry in her heart forever, a herd of elephants emerged from the treeline and waded into the river, the calves splashing and playing while the adults drank.

Sarah had no words. She simply watched, her hand finding David's, her heart full to bursting.

"This is why I do what I do," David said softly. "Not for the photographs, not for the fame or the money. For moments like this. Moments that remind me that the world is still full of wonder."

On the second day, they visited a Maasai village, a cluster of traditional manyattas surrounded by a thorn-bush fence. The village had clearly received visitors before — there was a small area set aside for tourism, with crafts laid out on blankets — but the welcome was genuine, the hospitality warm.

The elders greeted them with a traditional dance. The warriors formed a line and began to sing, their voices deep and rhythmic, rising in a call-and-response pattern that seemed to echo from the hills themselves. One by one, they leaped into the air, their bodies rising with astonishing height, their red shukas flying, the earth seeming to push them higher with each bound. The adamu — the jumping dance — was a display of strength and vitality, a celebration of Maasai manhood.

Sarah watched in wonder, David beside her, his camera clicking softly as he captured the movement, the joy, the sheer vitality of the dancers.

"They're jumping to show their strength," David explained. "The higher they jump, the more impressive they are. It's part of the courtship tradition."

"Like a mating dance," Sarah said, smiling.

"Exactly like a mating dance. Humans aren't so different from animals, when you strip away the pretense."

After the dance, Sarah was invited into the women's circle. The elder women gathered around her, their faces weathered and kind, their heads shaved, their necks adorned with layers of colorful beads. They sat on the ground, and Sarah sat with them, feeling suddenly shy.

One of the women — she introduced herself as Naserian — took Sarah's hands in hers. Her skin was warm and rough, calloused from years of work. "You are far from home," she said in English, the words careful but clear.

"Yes. Very far."

"Your heart is heavy. I can see it."

Sarah felt tears prick at her eyes. "Yes."

Naserian smiled. "The heart is like the sky. Sometimes it fills with clouds. But the clouds pass. The sun always returns."

She taught Sarah a song. The other women joined in, their voices rising in harmony, their hands gesturing to the earth and the sky and the children playing nearby. The melody was simple, repetitive, but it seemed to carry the weight of generations.

"The song is about connection," Naserian explained. "It says, 'We are the earth, and the earth is us. We are the sky, and the sky is us. We are each other, and we are one.'"

Sarah felt the truth of those words settle into her bones. She sang along, her voice tentative at first, then growing stronger. The women smiled, and Naserian squeezed her hands.

"You are learning," she said. "That is good."

On the third day, David took her to see the wildebeest migration.

They drove to a crossing point on the Mara River, where thousands upon thousands of wildebeests and zebras were massed on the banks. The air was filled with the sound of hooves and snorts and the calls of the animals. The ground trembled beneath them, a constant vibration that Sarah felt in her chest.

"The crossing," David said. "It happens when the animals decide it's time. Not when it's safe. Not when it's easy. When the moment demands it."

They waited for hours. The animals milled and jostled, a sea of bodies stretching in every direction. And then, without warning, a single wildebeest stepped into the water.

The crossing was chaos and terror and beauty all at once. The animals surged into the river, their bodies thundering through the water, their eyes wide with fear and determination. Crocodiles emerged from the depths, taking their share. The weak fell and were trampled. The strong pressed on.

And on the other side, the survivors climbed the bank and shook themselves dry and walked on into the waiting grass, their journey continuing, their purpose unchanged.

Sarah watched with tears streaming down her face.

"Do you understand now?" David asked.

"Yes," she said. "I understand."

The crossing was not about safety. It was about courage. The courage to step into the unknown, to trust that the journey was worth the risk, to believe that on the other side, there was something worth reaching.

The courage to choose.

## Chapter 13 — The Call from London

On the fourth morning, as they were packing to leave the Mara, Sarah's phone rang.

She looked at the screen and felt her stomach drop.

It was Robert.

She stepped away from the camp, walking to a spot where the grass rose around her like a golden sea. She took a deep breath and answered.

"Hi."

"Sarah. Finally. I've been trying to reach you for two days."

"I've been in the Maasai Mara. There's no signal."

"The Maasai Mara? Since when do you go on safari?"

"Since —" She stopped, unsure how much to tell him. "Since I finished the project early. A friend offered to show me around."

"A friend? Who?"

"Someone I met on the plane, Robert. A photographer. He's been showing me the country."

There was a long pause on the other end of the line.

"A photographer," Robert repeated, his voice flat. "A man you met on a plane. And you're in the middle of nowhere with him."

"It's not like that —"

"Isn't it? Because I've been calling you for two days, Sarah. Two days. I was worried sick. I thought something had happened to you. And now I find out you're on some romantic getaway with a stranger."

"It's not a romantic getaway —"

"Don't lie to me." His voice was sharp now, cutting through the static. "I can hear it in your voice. Something's different. Something's changed."

Sarah closed her eyes. The sun was warm on her face, and the wind carried the scent of grass and earth, and she could see David standing by the Land Rover, watching her with concern in his eyes.

"You're right," she said quietly. "Something has changed."

Robert was silent.

"I don't know how to explain this," she continued. "I don't know how to make you understand something I don't fully understand myself. But I've been thinking — for a long time — and I don't think I can marry you."

The words hung in the air between them, across continents and time zones and the vast distance that had always existed between what she felt and what she had pretended to feel.

"Is this a joke?"

"No, Robert. It's not a joke."

"Because of some man you met on a plane? You're throwing away three years for someone you've known for two weeks?"

"Robert, listen to me. This isn't about him. He's — he's part of it, yes. But he's not the reason. The reason is that I've been lying to myself for three years. I've been telling myself that I love you the way a woman should love the man she's going to marry. But I don't. I care about you. I respect you. But I'm not in love with you. I don't think I ever was."

The silence stretched, heavy and painful.

"Robert?"

"Don't. Just — don't." His voice was thick with something — anger, hurt, both. "I have to go."

The line went dead.

Sarah lowered the phone and stared at the empty screen. She felt hollow, emptied of everything but the certainty that she had done the right thing — and the terror of what came next.

David appeared beside her, his steps quiet on the grass.

"Are you okay?"

"I just ended my engagement."

He didn't say anything. He simply took her hand and held it, standing with her in the vastness of the Mara, a silent witness to the ending of one story and the beginning of another.

## Chapter 14 — David's Fear

They drove back to Nairobi in near-silence, the weight of what had happened pressing down on them both. Sarah stared out the window, watching the landscape change from the golden plains of the Mara to the green hills of the Rift Valley to the congested streets of the city.

When they reached the Serena Hotel, David parked the Land Rover and turned to her.

"Do you want me to come in?"

"I think I need to be alone tonight. There's a lot I need to process."

"Of course." He reached out and touched her face, his fingers gentle against her cheek. "I'm here, Sarah. Whatever you need."

"Thank you. For everything."

She leaned in and kissed him — a brief, soft kiss that carried all the things they hadn't said.

Then she got out of the car and walked into the hotel, feeling his eyes on her until the doors closed behind her.

David sat in the parking lot for a long time after she left.

He should have felt elated. She had ended her engagement. She had chosen — not him, not yet, but she had taken a step toward choosing herself. Toward choosing a life that was truly her own.

But instead of elation, he felt fear.

The old fear. The familiar fear. The stone in his pocket that his mother had told him to let go.

What if he wasn't enough?

What if, when the initial excitement faded, Sarah realized that he was just a photographer with a battered Land Rover and a rented apartment and a life that was too transient for someone like her?

What if she woke up one day and decided — like Amara had — that he was not the man she needed him to be?

He drove home in a daze, the streets of Nairobi blurring past his windows. A matatu cut him off at a roundabout, and he didn't even honk. A hawker approached his window at a traffic light, offering a carved wooden giraffe, and David waved him away without meeting his eyes.

He climbed the stairs to his apartment — the same stairs he had climbed for five years, the same creaking third step, the same loose railing. He had always told himself this was temporary, that he would find a better place when he had more money, more stability. But he had never tried. He had never allowed himself to want more than temporary.

He sat on his bed, his head in his hands. The room was quiet except for the hum of the refrigerator and the distant sound of traffic on Thika Road. The afternoon light filtered through his curtains, casting long shadows across the floor.

He thought about the things he hadn't told Sarah. That sometimes he woke up in the middle of the night, gasping, the dream of Amara's leaving playing on repeat in his mind. That he had spent years building walls so high that now he didn't know how to tear them down. That the fear of being left had become a prophecy he kept fulfilling by never letting anyone close enough to stay.

And yet — Sarah had ended her engagement for him. She had chosen uncertainty over safety. She had looked at the carefully ordered life she had built in London and decided it wasn't enough.

Could he do the same?

The photographs from the Mara were still on his camera, waiting to be downloaded and edited. He plugged the memory card into his laptop and watched the images appear on his screen. The lion at sunset, the elephants at the river crossing, the Maasai women dancing. And Sarah — Sarah in the golden light, Sarah with her face turned to the sun, Sarah laughing. He stopped at one image: her profile against the vast sky, her expression one of pure, unguarded wonder. He had captured her soul in that moment.

He looked at the cardboard box under his bed.

He had not opened it in days. He had not thought about Amara in days.

For the first time in years, the past had loosened its grip on him. But the future — the future was a vast, terrifying unknown, and he did not know if he had the courage to step into it.

He picked up the box. He carried it to the kitchen. He opened it one last time, looked at the photograph of Amara, remembered the woman she had been and the man he had been with her. Then he tore the photograph in half. And again. And again, until the pieces were too small to recognize.

He dropped them into the trash bin and washed his hands.

It was time to let go.

It was time to be brave.

His phone buzzed.

A message from Sarah: *I am not asking you to be anything other than who you are.*

He stared at the words, his heart racing.

*I am not looking for certainty,* she continued. *I am looking for someone who is brave enough to try.*

He typed back: *What if I don't know how to be brave?*

Her reply came almost immediately: *Then I will show you.*

## Chapter 15 — The Choice

The next few days were a blur of decisions and conversations.

Sarah called her mother, who reacted with the predictable mix of shock and disappointment.

"You are throwing away a good man," her mother said, her voice tight with emotion. "Robert has a good job, a good family. He treats you well. What more do you want?"

"I want to be happy, Mama. Truly happy. Not just comfortable."

"And this photographer — he will make you happy? A man who lives in a rented apartment and spends his life traveling to dangerous places?"

"I don't know if he will make me happy. But when I'm with him, I feel like I'm waking up from a long sleep. I feel like myself — the self I was before I started trying to be what everyone else wanted me to be."

There was a long silence on the other end of the line.

"*Mwana wange,*" her mother said finally — *my child.* "I wanted you to have a safe life. A good life. I wanted to protect you from the struggles I had. But perhaps — perhaps I was trying to protect you from the wrong things."

"Perhaps."

"Be careful, Sarah. Follow your heart. But be careful."

"I will, Mama."

Sarah called Robert's mother, who cried. She called Robert's sister, who was furious. She called her boss at the firm, who was surprisingly supportive. "I've been where you are," her boss said. "I chose the safe path. Sometimes I wonder what would have happened if I hadn't."

She called Grace, who laughed. "I knew it. I saw it coming from the day we had dinner."

"Did you?"

"You have the look, Sarah. The look of a woman who has finally found what she was looking for."

And she called David.

"I need to see you," she said.

"I'm at my apartment. Come over."

She took a taxi to Kitisuru, navigating the narrow, unpaved roads until they reached David's building. It was a modest place — a ground-floor flat in a block of four, the paint faded, the garden overgrown. But when David opened the door, and she saw his face, it felt like the most beautiful place in the world.

"Come in," he said.

His apartment was small but filled with light. The walls were covered in photographs — lions and elephants and landscapes, portraits of Maasai warriors and children laughing and old women with weathered faces. There was a worn couch, a wooden coffee table covered in photography books, and a small balcony that overlooked a patch of garden.

"This is you," she said, looking at the photographs. "This is your soul, displayed on these walls."

"Showing someone your work is like showing them your diary," David said. "But I wanted you to see."

She turned to face him.

"I ended my engagement. I told my mother. I told my boss. I've started the process of untangling my life from the future I was supposed to have."

"And how do you feel?"

"Terrified. And free. I feel like I've been holding my breath for years, and I'm finally allowed to breathe."

David stepped closer, his hands finding hers.

"I'm scared too," he said. "I'm scared that I'll fail you. That I'll be the same man I was with Amara — too caught up in my work, too afraid to commit. I want to be different. I want to be the man you deserve. But I don't know if I know how."

"Then we learn together." Sarah squeezed his hands. "I'm not Amara. You're not the man you were with her. We're new people, David. This is a new story. And I want to write it with you."

He kissed her then — a deep, searching kiss that said everything words couldn't. When they pulled apart, his eyes were wet.

"I don't know what comes next," he said. "I don't know how this works — you in London, me in Nairobi, the distance, the complications."

"Neither do I. But I know that I don't want to walk away from this. I know that I want to try."

They stood together in his small apartment, surrounded by photographs of a world he had captured with his camera, and made a pact — not a promise, not a plan, but a commitment to being brave enough to see where this journey would take them.

## Chapter 16 — Goodbyes

Sarah's last week in Nairobi came too quickly.

She spent her days at the museum site, finalizing details and saying goodbye to the workers she had come to know. She spent her evenings with David, eating at small restaurants, walking through the city streets, lying in his bed with the windows open, listening to the sounds of Nairobi at night.

They knew the end was coming. They tried not to think about it.

On her last day, David picked her up from the hotel and drove her to a place she had not seen — the Nairobi Arboretum, a forest of indigenous trees in the heart of the city. They walked along the paths, the canopy of leaves dappling the sunlight, the air filled with birdsong.

"I have something for you," David said.

He handed her a small package, wrapped in brown paper.

She opened it carefully. Inside was a photograph — a print of the first moment she had seen the Mara, her face captured in profile, wonder written across her features. The light was golden, the background a blur of green and gold. In the corner, David had written: *The moment I knew.*

"David —" She felt tears prick at her eyes.

"I told you I would remember it forever. This way, you can too."

She threw her arms around him, holding him tightly, her face pressed against his chest.

"I don't want to leave," she whispered.

"I know. I don't want you to leave either."

"But I have to. The project is done. My life is in London."

"For now."

She pulled back and looked at him, a question in her eyes.

"For now," she repeated.

"I'm not letting you go, Sarah. I don't know how this will work, but I'm not letting you go. We'll figure it out. We'll find a way."

At the airport, they stood at the departure gate, surrounded by the chaos of travelers and the announcements of flights. The moment stretched, fragile and precious.

"Call me when you land," David said.

"I will."

"Don't forget about the Mara."

"How could I?"

"And don't forget about me."

She reached up and touched his face, tracing the line of his jaw with her fingers.

"I could never forget about you, David Ochieng. You're in my bones now."

He kissed her one last time — a kiss that tasted of salt and promise and the ache of goodbye.

Then she turned and walked through the gate, not looking back, because if she looked back, she wouldn't be able to go.

## Chapter 17 — London Interlude

London felt different.

Sarah had expected it to feel the same — the gray skies, the crowded streets, the familiar rhythm of her life. But everything had shifted, rearranged itself in her absence. The flat she shared with Robert was now just her flat. Her routine was now hers alone.

The first morning, she woke up in the bed she had shared with Robert for three years and felt the emptiness beside her not as a loss but as a release. She made coffee — Kenyan coffee she had brought back from Nairobi — and sat at the kitchen table, looking out at the gray London sky, and felt the silence settle around her like a new garment that didn't quite fit yet.

She spent the first week in a daze, unpacking and repacking, going through the motions of a life that no longer fit. She removed Robert's things from the flat — photographs, books, a suit he had left in the closet, his collection of vintage vinyl records that he had insisted on displaying on the living room shelf. She boxed them up and sent them to his office with a note that said nothing more than *I'm sorry.*

When the boxes were gone, the flat felt larger. Emptier. She walked from room to room, running her fingers over surfaces that still held traces of their shared life. A dent in the sofa where Robert always sat. A scratch on the kitchen counter from the time he had tried to open a bottle with a knife. The faint smell of his cologne, lingering in the bedroom.

She had expected grief. She had expected regret. Instead, she felt a quiet relief, like the lifting of a weight she had been carrying so long she had forgotten it was there.

Her friend Priya came over on the third day, bearing a bottle of wine and a container of homemade samosas. Priya was a graphic designer she had met in architecture school, the kind of friend who told you the truth even when you didn't want to hear it.

"So," Priya said, settling onto the sofa with her wine. "You ended your engagement to a successful banker and threw yourself at a photographer you met on a plane."

"When you say it like that, it sounds insane."

"It is insane. It's also the most Sarah thing you've ever done."

"What does that mean?"

Priya took a long sip of wine. "It means you've always played it safe. You chose the safe career, the safe man, the safe life. And you were miserable — I could see it. We could all see it. But Sarah Nakato doesn't do reckless things. Until now."

"So you think I made the right choice?"

"I think you made a choice. Whether it's right or wrong — that's up to you to decide. But at least you're choosing now. That's more than you were doing before."

Sarah threw herself into work, spending long hours at the firm, reviewing the final plans for the Kazuri Museum. The project was her anchor, her connection to Nairobi, to the place where her heart had come alive. She pored over the blueprints, the material samples, the color palettes. She video-called Grace twice a week to check on the construction progress. The museum was rising from the red earth, and so was she.

Every night, she video-called David.

They talked for hours — about their days, their thoughts, the million small details that made up their separate lives. He showed her the photographs he had taken, and she showed him her drawings. They read books together, taking turns reading aloud. They fell asleep with the calls still connected, waking to the sound of each other's breathing.

"Distance is hard," Sarah said one night, lying in her bed, the phone propped beside her pillow.

"Yes," David agreed. "But it's also a test."

"A test of what?"

"Of how much we want this. If we can survive the distance, we can survive anything."

"Do you think we can?"

"I know we can," he said. "I've never been more certain of anything."

But the certainty was not unshakeable. There were nights when Sarah lay awake, staring at the ceiling, wondering if she had made a terrible mistake. She had given up a stable relationship, a comfortable future, the approval of her family and friends — all for a man she had known for two weeks.

And yet.

When she closed her eyes, she saw the Mara. She saw the lion and the river crossing and the sunset from the kopje. She saw David's face when he looked at her, as if she was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

She had felt more alive in those two weeks in Kenya than she had in three years with Robert. How could that be a mistake?

*It's not a mistake,* she told herself. *It's the first real choice I've made in years.*

## Chapter 18 — The Visit

Six weeks after Sarah returned to London, David called with news.

"I have an assignment," he said. "A travel magazine wants a photo essay on London. Three weeks, all expenses paid."

"David —"

"They're putting me up in a flat in Shoreditch. I'll be there from the first of November."

Sarah felt her heart leap. "You're coming to London?"

"I'm coming to London."

The weeks leading up to his visit were the longest of her life. She prepared the flat, cleaned every corner, bought fresh flowers for the bedroom. She was nervous, excited, terrified. What if the connection they had forged in Kenya didn't translate to London? What if the reality of their relationship couldn't survive the context of her everyday life?

The day of his arrival, she took the train to Heathrow. She waited at the arrivals gate, her heart pounding, scanning the faces emerging from the customs hall.

And then she saw him.

He was wearing a simple white shirt and dark jeans, his camera bag slung over his shoulder. He looked tired from the flight, but when he saw her, his face lit up with a smile that made her knees weak.

"Sarah."

"David."

They embraced, and it felt like coming home.

The next three weeks were a revelation.

David photographed London — the Thames at dawn, the gardens of Kensington, the markets of East London, the crowds at Trafalgar Square. But his best photographs were of her. He captured her laughing at a café, her hair wild in the wind on the South Bank, her face soft in the morning light as she slept.

"It's not just about beautiful places," he told her. "It's about the person you're with. The Mara was beautiful, but you made it unforgettable. London is the same."

He met her colleagues at a dinner party and charmed them with his stories of lions and leopards and Maasai warriors. He met her friend Priya over coffee and they talked for hours about his photography. He even spoke to her mother on the phone, his Luo politeness and gentle voice winning her over.

"He seems like a good man," her mother admitted afterward. "Different from Robert. But perhaps different is what you need."

On his last night in London, they sat on the roof of her flat, looking out at the city lights, a bottle of wine between them.

"I don't want to go," David said.

"Then stay."

"I can't. I have commitments. A project in Tanzania starting in December. A workshop in Cape Town in January."

"Can I come with you?"

He turned to look at her, surprised. "What about your work?"

"I've been thinking about it," Sarah said. "The museum is opening in March. I've been looking at opportunities in East Africa. There are architectural firms in Nairobi. There are projects I could work on. I don't have to be in London."

David set down his wine glass and took her hands.

"Are you serious?"

"I've never been more serious about anything. I want to be with you, David. I want to try. Really try. Not just phone calls and visits. A real life together."

"Your whole life is here."

"My old life was here. My new life — I want to build it with you. In Africa. Where we both belong."

He kissed her, and the city lights blurred around them, and they held each other as the cold November wind blew across the rooftop.

"Come to Tanzania," he said. "Come with me in December. We'll figure out the rest together."

"Yes," she said. "Yes."

## Chapter 19 — The Crossing

The airplane descended through the clouds, and below them, the landscape of Tanzania emerged — the green of the coastal plain, the blue of the Indian Ocean, the distant peak of Kilimanjaro shrouded in mist.

Sarah pressed her face to the window, her heart full.

She had done it. She had sold her flat, quit her job, stored her belongings, and said goodbye to London. Her colleagues had thrown her a farewell party. Her mother had cried. Her brother had hugged her and told her she was brave.

And now she was here, sitting beside David, flying toward an uncertain future that felt more certain than anything she had ever known.

"This is the crossing," David said, echoing the words he had spoken in the Mara. "The moment when you step into the water and trust that the current won't sweep you away."

"I'm ready," she said.

And she was.

They spent a month in Tanzania, and it was everything.

They started in Arusha, at the foot of Mount Meru, where Sarah had her first taste of Tanzanian coffee — richer and darker than the Kenyan brew she had grown to love. They stayed at a small guesthouse run by a German woman who had married a Tanzanian and never left. The garden was bursting with frangipani and bougainvillea, and in the mornings, colobus monkeys leaped through the trees above their breakfast table.

From Arusha, they drove south to Tarangire National Park, where the baobab trees stood like ancient sentinels against the sky. David spent hours photographing the elephants that gathered at the Tarangire River — family groups with calves no bigger than her hip, their ears flapping lazily in the afternoon heat. Sarah sat in the shade of a baobab, sketching the curve of its trunk, the texture of its bark, the way the light filtered through its branches.

"You're drawing the same tree from three different angles," David observed, coming to sit beside her.

"It's a different tree each time I look at it," she said. "The light changes. My perspective changes. I change."

"That's the most architectural thing you've ever said."

She laughed and threw a blade of grass at him.

From Tarangire, they went to the Serengeti. The name meant "endless plains" in the Maasai language, and Sarah understood why the first time she saw them — a landscape so vast it seemed to go on forever, the grass moving in waves like a golden sea. They stayed in a mobile camp, the tents following the movement of the wildlife, the nights filled with the sounds of animals calling to each other in the darkness.

On their third night in the Serengeti, a pride of lions passed within fifty meters of their tent. Sarah heard the low rumble of their voices, the soft pad of their paws on the earth, and she held her breath, her hand finding David's in the darkness.

"Are they dangerous?" she whispered.

"Only if you're a zebra," David whispered back. "We're not on their menu."

In the morning, they found the tracks — massive paw prints pressed into the soft earth around their tent, a silent testament to the closeness of the wild.

They ended their journey at Lake Tanganyika, on the western border of Tanzania, where the water was so clear she could see fish swimming twenty feet below the surface. They rented a small boat and spent a day on the lake, stopping at deserted beaches where they swam naked in the warm water and dried themselves on sun-heated rocks.

"I could stay here forever," Sarah said, lying on a rock, her eyes closed, the sun warming her skin.

"Forever is a long time."

"I know. But I'd like to try."

David lay down beside her, his hand finding hers. "We can try. We can try forever."

They stayed in camps and lodges and small guesthouses. They ate together under the stars. They made love in the soft light of lanterns. They argued about small things — where to eat, which road to take, whether to drive at night — and reconciled with laughter and apologies and the willingness to try again. They built the foundation of a life together, brick by brick, moment by moment.

And at the end of the month, they flew to Nairobi.

Sarah had found a job with a firm that specialized in sustainable African architecture. She had rented a small house in the Karen neighborhood, with a garden and a view of the Ngong Hills. She had started the process of making Kenya her home.

David had taken a smaller apartment in the same neighborhood, though they spent most nights together, the divide between their two homes becoming increasingly theoretical.

On a Sunday morning, six months after their journey began, they drove to the Kazuri Museum for its grand opening.

The museum was magnificent.

The building rose from the red earth like a natural formation, its curves and angles echoing the landscape around it. The stone glowed warm in the morning light. The gardens were in bloom, the pathways lined with indigenous plants. A crowd had gathered for the ceremony — dignitaries and artists and schoolchildren, all come to celebrate the opening of a space dedicated to the beauty and complexity of East African culture.

Sarah stood at the entrance, tears streaming down her face.

"You did this," David said, his arm around her shoulders.

"We did this. Africa did this."

Grace appeared, a huge smile on her face, dragging her three children behind her. "Sarah! You came!"

"Of course I came. I wouldn't miss this for the world."

They embraced, laughing and crying at the same time.

"You look happy," Grace said.

"I am happy."

"More than happy. You look alive."

Sarah looked at David, at the museum, at the crowd of people gathered to celebrate the creation they had built together.

"I am," she said. "I finally am."

## Chapter 20 — Epilogue: Two Years Later

The wedding was small.

It took place on a hill overlooking the Maasai Mara, in the golden light of a late afternoon, with only a handful of people present. Sarah's mother and brother had flown in from Kampala. David's mother had come from Nairobi, her eyes bright with tears of joy. Grace was there with her family, her husband John having finally taken time off from his safari guiding to stand beside her. Lekuta, the Maasai watchman who had welcomed them on their first visit, stood as a witness.

They had chosen the Mara because it was where their love had been born — where Sarah had first heard a lion roar, where they had kissed on a kopje under the stars, where David had photographed her face in the golden light and known, with absolute certainty, that he wanted to spend the rest of his life making her smile.

The ceremony was conducted by a Maasai elder, a man named Ole Nkurumah who had known David since he was a young photographer visiting the Mara for the first time. He blessed their union with words in Maa that David translated for Sarah afterward: "May your love be like the acacia tree — deep-rooted, wide-branched, and strong enough to weather any storm."

Sarah wore a dress of cream-colored silk, simple and elegant, the kind of dress she had never imagined for herself. It had been made by a tailor in Nairobi, a woman who specialized in modern African fashion, and the fabric was patterned with subtle geometric designs inspired by the traditional art of the Luo people — David's tribe. Around her neck, she wore a necklace that David had given her the night before: a small silver pendant shaped like an acacia tree, its branches reaching toward the sky.

She wore no diamond ring — instead, David had given her a band of beaded leather, crafted by the women of the village they had visited on their first trip to the Mara. Naserian herself had made it, the beads forming a pattern of two intertwining paths that merged into one.

They wrote their own vows.

"You showed me the Mara," Sarah said, her voice steady despite the tears running down her face. "You showed me what it means to be alive, truly alive, in a world that teaches us to settle for safety. You taught me to cross the river, even when I was afraid. I promise to keep crossing with you, for the rest of my life."

David took her hands in his. He was wearing a simple white shirt and a blue blazer, and his eyes were wet with tears he didn't bother to hide.

"Before I met you, I was a photograph — a single moment, frozen in time. You taught me to move again. You taught me that love is not a cage but a door. I promise to walk through every door with you, to face every sunrise and every storm, to be by your side for as long as the Mara exists."

When they kissed, the crowd cheered, and somewhere in the distance, a lion roared.

The reception was a feast under the stars, the food cooked over open fires — nyama choma and ugali and sukuma wiki, dishes that tasted of Sarah's childhood in Kampala and David's in Nairobi and the shared heritage of East Africa. The music was provided by the villagers who had come to celebrate, a mix of traditional songs and modern Kenyan pop that had everyone dancing.

Sarah danced with her mother, who held her tight and whispered, "I was wrong, *mwana wange.* This — this is what I wanted for you. Not safety. This."

"You wanted me to be happy, Mama. That's all any mother wants."

"I thought safety was happiness. I was wrong."

"You weren't wrong. You just didn't know there was another way."

Sarah danced with her brother, who told her she looked beautiful and that he had always known she would find her own path. She danced with Grace, who laughed and cried and said she had known from the very first dinner that Sarah would end up here. She danced with David's mother, a small woman with a powerful grip and eyes that held a lifetime of wisdom.

"My son loves you," David's mother said, her voice thick with emotion. "I have not seen him look at anyone the way he looks at you. Not since —" She stopped, shook her head. "Not ever. He has never looked at anyone the way he looks at you."

"I love him too, Mama."

"I know. I can see it. You are good for him. You make him brave."

Finally, Sarah danced with David. The music had slowed, a Swahili love song playing from a small speaker, and they moved together in the firelight, the night sky glittering above them like a promise written in stars.

"What happens now?" she asked, her head resting against his chest.

"Now we live," he said. "We build our house. We take photographs. We design buildings. We travel. We come home. We love each other, every day, for the rest of our lives."

"Promise?"

"I promise."

He kissed her, and the stars wheeled above them, and the wind carried the scent of grass and freedom and forever.

They crossed mountains and rivers and the boundaries of their own hearts. They are proof that love is the strongest force in the world."

"Tell us more," one of the children said.

And so Lekuta told them the whole story — of how the builder had come from across the water, carrying a heart heavy with duty, and how the photographer had been trapped in the amber of his own past. Of how they had found each other on a metal bird in the sky, and how the Mara had taught them what it meant to be truly alive.

The children listened with wide eyes, and when the story was finished, they looked up at the stars and wondered what adventures awaited them.

Far away, in a small house in Karen, a house that Sarah and David had bought together and filled with photographs and blueprints and the accumulated evidence of a life built together, a new story had begun to unfold. Sarah was pregnant — they had discovered it two weeks before the wedding — and the future stretched before them like the endless plains of the Serengeti, full of possibility and light.
